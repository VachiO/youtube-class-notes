As you probably remember, the professor talked about the difficulty of finding causes, especially the search for what we call causality. Cause and effect, or "causality" in English, was discussed for the whole previous session.

During that session, the teacher told a story about people linking an environmental condition to the giraffe. They said the giraffe struggled to survive and altered itself by making its neck longer so it could eat food high in the trees. In other words, the environment and the trees were treated as the cause that made the giraffe's neck long. The person associated with this idea was the scientist Charles Darwin, whose name students have probably heard before.

The teacher explained, however, that it is not simply that a giraffe stretches or changes its neck during its own lifetime. A giraffe is born with its neck. National Geographic once organized a group to investigate whether giraffes really acquired long necks or had them from birth, and the finding was that they had them from birth. The professor used this as a cautionary example: even with things we can see with our own eyes, people sometimes connect two things without thinking carefully about whether that causal connection is true.

Another case occurred in the United States around the 1960s. There was a belief that household cooking gas was the reason so many Americans died by suicide—more than 5,000 in one year. Household gas at that time differed from today's natural gas. The older gas contained carbon monoxide. Because it had no strong smell or irritating odor, inhaling it directly could make a person unconscious within ten or fifteen minutes and then cause death. This made it an easy means of suicide.

When we hear the argument that cooking gas "caused" more than 5,000 deaths, however, we have to be careful. Gas was not necessarily the root cause of suicide. People may have had depression, illness, or many other problems. The gas was a facilitator that made the act easier. It is therefore misleading to say that gas itself was the main cause of all those deaths.

The same issue appeared in San Francisco after the Golden Gate Bridge was completed. Some people jumped from the bridge and ended their lives, so one could say, "It is all because of that bridge; once it was created, people flocked there to jump." That sounds like a causal explanation, but it is not the whole truth. There was debate about raising the bridge railings to prevent people from jumping, as reported in San Francisco news. The bridge may have made the act more accessible or easier, just as gas did, but a person who does such a thing has other underlying reasons. The bridge itself is not the complete cause.

The teacher said that analysis of cause and effect must follow principles. From last week's examples through today's lesson, we need to identify the underlying principles and the components required to provide a sound answer.

The professor briefly mentioned feeling unwell and coughing, and needing permission to receive an injection or medicinal spray.

For causal analysis, four things are needed. First, we need data. Second, we need a framework or perspective, called a conceptual framework in English. Third, we need a method, or methodology. Thai textbooks often translate methodology as "research methodology," which can make it sound complicated. The professor explained it simply: methodology is the method or way of studying something. Once we have information and have identified a case or an actor, methodology tells us how to study that case further in order to reach a conclusion. Some call it scientific methodology or use other terms, but in simple language it is the way we study something.

The professor will explain each component in detail. Fourth, after we have data, a conceptual framework, and a method of study, we still need an answer. We are looking for cause and effect, so what technique will we use to draw a conclusion? How will we determine whether what we predicted was correct, whether events occurred as expected, and whether our assumptions were actually supported? The third and fourth components may sound complicated, but the professor will explain them step by step and element by element until they lead us toward an answer.

The discussion begins with data. Everyone understands the general meaning of data, but consider a question such as: Are students satisfied with the professor's teaching? There must be data showing whether students are satisfied. In a classroom or online session with about fifty people, the professor might distribute a questionnaire asking about satisfaction with the teaching method.

Even that question may be too broad. To answer a broad question properly, it should be broken down point by point: Was the professor prepared? Was the content explained in an easy-to-understand way? Was the communication clear? Those specific responses become the information needed to answer the intended question and to provide the knowledge students want about the topic.

The fourth point, in ordinary language, is to answer the questions the professor intends to answer or provide the knowledge students want about the subject. There are ways of finding the information mentioned earlier. One example is conducting a survey and distributing questionnaires to students for evaluation. We call data collected directly in this way primary data.

Primary data is first-hand, preliminary, or raw data. Last time the professor mentioned the population census. A census is a survey of all people and households throughout the country. It is not random sampling in which a smaller sample is selected and then used to represent the whole country. A population census, called a census in English, is based on factual data collected directly. The student-evaluation responses in the earlier example are also primary data.

We need to understand the nature of primary data or primary sources. They are raw facts: How many people are there in Thailand? How many are women and how many are men? What are their age ranges? Today the country has more detailed age categories because the population is entering an aging-society era. Surveys of elderly people's health and medical treatment are examples of primary data.

The teacher then gave an example of a factual claim: democracy is a political system commonly used by countries around the world to govern themselves. That statement is very broad. After hearing it, we have to ask whether we should believe it and whether it is true. Is democracy really the form of government widely used around the world? We must first find factual information, perhaps a survey of the systems of government used by different countries, to determine which system is most prevalent.

Students should develop the habit of thinking this way. When we listen to people speak on television, online media, or elsewhere, speakers who present data are more engaging and credible. Their information builds the listener's trust because it shows that the speaker has evidence. One presentation technique the professor recommends is therefore to ask, whenever we present something, whether we have data.

Suppose someone suddenly says that Thailand currently has a very high divorce rate. We should listen carefully and ask what "very high" means. Is the current divorce rate higher than in previous years, or is Thailand's rate high compared with rates in other countries? The speaker must know what comparison is being made and have information to support it. The listener also has to stay focused and ask whether the person is merely speaking casually or has a valid evidentiary basis.

In meetings, whenever someone makes a claim, the professor often asks to see the supporting information. Very often the speaker is presenting personal emotion and feeling. When asked whether there is actual information, the answer is no. More than half the time, people prefer to express opinions rather than present facts.

If we want to be credible speakers, we need to have the information at hand. Without it, extensive questioning can expose the weakness of our claim. Some people insert numbers into a presentation to create credibility. A politician in a parliamentary debate, for example, may cite a figure such as 38.912 percent for a ministry budget or say that the Ministry of Education represents a particular share of the total budget. A percentage with many decimal places sounds extraordinarily precise. But a diligent listener will check whether that 38.912 percent is actually accurate. A fabricated number may initially make the speaker appear trustworthy, but it is not trustworthy once someone verifies it.

Donald Trump is a political leader who often speaks in numerical generalizations. He has claimed that people entering the United States illegally, many from Latin America through the southern border, are criminals or thieves and must be arrested and removed from the country. When a leader says something, the statement functions like a signal to officials and operators, who may then act according to that belief and opinion.

The agency carrying out immigration-control work is ICE, an abbreviation for Immigration and Customs Enforcement. It is a government agency responsible for immigration and customs enforcement. Under Trump, ICE has been widely criticized in the United States. General claims that most immigrants entered illegally or are criminals can lead officers to inspect places, make arrests, and use force without adequately checking the facts. Some people arrested have been citizens or have already received citizenship, and even Americans have been detained or removed in error.

The leader's words therefore act as a signal for enforcement officials, and recent incidents in many places have involved violent methods and judgments made without considering factual data. That is dangerous. When making a speech or presenting something, we must be careful not to generalize without evidence, because such generalizations shape a mindset and can cause officials to act as if everyone who came to the United States were an unlawful outsider.

Returning to the lesson, remember the first requirement: to analyze anything, we need information. The professor's point is that there must be evidence of what Trump actually said; people should not simply believe baseless rumors. One type of data is primary data, meaning information we receive directly. Another type is secondary data. Secondary data are information that other people have already compiled, edited, categorized, or analyzed.

Political science often discusses the middle class. If we want to know what percentage of Thailand's population is middle class, we cannot answer until we define whom the term "middle class" refers to and what criteria are used to measure it. Only then can we say whether the figure is 50 percent, 55 percent, 60 percent, or some other percentage. Those compiled and analyzed figures are secondary data. They are information at another level beyond the original raw data.

Consider another example. We may ask what percentage of students registered in a course and eventually graduated. The initial counts are numerical data. If all registered students are treated as 100 percent and approximately 30 percent graduate, we then want to understand why the remaining 70 percent did not graduate. Researchers must analyze the numbers.

At Ramkhamhaeng University, not everyone enrolls solely with the intention of completing a degree. Some people enroll to gain knowledge, such as taking Civil and Commercial Law because they want to understand that subject. Tuition is inexpensive. Therefore, a large number of students not graduating does not mean that the students lack intelligence. Many people study because they want knowledge. Others do intend to graduate: they may be young people who recently completed school, or people already in the labor market who need to complete a degree to improve their qualifications or career situation.

Percentages produced through analysis and synthesis are secondary data. Secondary data naturally follow from primary data. They are information constructed or processed to meet particular needs, and they can be used for further study. For example, we may want to know what role or political influence the middle class has, whether access to information is easier for that group, or whether it makes rational political decisions.

There are thus two initial categories: primary data and secondary data. How the information should be used depends on its nature. Because data are often collected, studied, modified, regrouped, and used for a particular purpose, the information may shift away from its original context or become inaccurate. A good researcher who wants a logical answer needs to know the background and source of the data.

When the professor reads a research paper, he likes to look first at the data rather than immediately reading the author's prose or summary. He examines tables, equations, and statistics with his own eyes and only afterward reads the text. Quite often, the author describes the information incorrectly and reaches a wrong conclusion. A reader may therefore form an interpretation different from the author's. The data can speak for themselves and may not support what the author expected. This is the benefit of examining tables and individual items before reading the narrative. Otherwise, authors may simply make things up and connect unrelated points.

The teacher then distinguished another pair of data types. One is empirical data. Examples include measurable claims about the age composition of a population—for instance, the percentage who are elderly and the percentage under twenty years old. These are observable, tangible, quantitative facts. "Empirical data" means information grounded in things we can see or observe: images, phenomena, protests, exam papers being submitted, or an answer sheet being altered.

The professor connected this to reports of examination cheating. Reporters must use precise language. An exam paper and an answer sheet are not the same thing. If the alleged act was altering an answer sheet, the report should say that, because saying that an exam paper was altered refers to something different. When making a claim, we must be careful about exactly what object or event we mean.

The teacher described two categories here: empirical data and non-empirical data. Empirical data include observable facts, figures, or images. Non-empirical matters may not provide numbers or pictures, although we may still be aware that a phenomenon is occurring.

A current issue causing argument concerns allegations of collusion in an election, meaning a mutual understanding or coordinated arrangement. Going to court without factual evidence will result in losing the case. Information is crucial even if there are no clear figures. We still have to ask what "collusion" refers to—for example, whether there were telephone conversations or some other observable evidence.
